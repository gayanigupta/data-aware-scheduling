\noindent\textbf{Abstract:}
Time-critical workflows---flood forecasting, wildfire tracking,
evacuation planning, offshore platform monitoring---run as
directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) of dependent tasks. These
workflows must produce answers under hard deadlines, exactly
when the wide-area link to the cloud is degraded or unavailable,
so the computation has to happen on fog resources federated
around the event. Scheduling in this setting is harder than in
the cloud: every edge of the DAG carries data that may have to
cross a slow link, and every task can trade result accuracy for
execution time. Existing schedulers treat these aspects in
isolation---or ignore them entirely---and almost all of them
assume the schedule is computed once, before execution, and
never revisited.

This research develops a three-part treatment of the problem.
First, we formulated the joint allocation problem---task
placement, per-edge data movement, per-link bandwidth, and
per-task approximate execution under a global deadline---as a
mixed-integer program with correctness proofs, and built three
scalable schedulers to solve it (published at IEEE Cloud Summit
2025). Second, we hardened that foundation into a data-aware,
measurable evaluation: a maximum-accuracy bound as a reference
for every method, a retry-once failure model separating accuracy
from feasibility, a bandwidth-degradation study exposing what
bandwidth-blind schedulers get wrong, and honest scalability
limits for each solver. Third, we propose to remove the static
assumption itself: when a whole fog resource fails mid-workflow,
the goal is to identify the smallest affected region of the task
DAG, repartition and reassign only that region onto surviving
resources, update only the communication paths that changed, and
resume execution without restarting completed work---recovering
faster with less disturbance than a complete repartition.

\section{Introduction}

Workflows that respond to disasters, such as flood forecasting,
wildfire tracking, and evacuation planning, run as directed
acyclic graphs (DAGs) of dependent tasks~\cite{yu2005taxonomy,
juve2013workflows}. A flood-response workflow, for example,
chains sensing, hydrological modeling, forecast computation,
evacuation planning, and alert dissemination. The deadline is
part of the specification: a forecast that arrives after the
water has risen is a forecast nobody uses.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{federated-fog-architecture.png}
\caption{The deployment setting. Sensors feed a response
workflow that must execute on fog sites federated around the
disaster area, because the uplink to the cloud is degraded or
lost precisely when the workflow is needed.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

These workloads cannot rely on the cloud. The same event that
creates the deadline---the flood, the fire, the platform
incident---degrades the wide-area network. The practical answer
is the compute continuum: edge devices acquire data, cloud and
HPC provide elastic capacity, and between them sit fog
resources---cell-tower racks, roadside cabinets, platform
servers---close enough to the event to matter~\cite{bonomi2012fog,
vaquero2014fog,shi2016edge,mouradian2018fog}. Individually a
fog site is too weak for the whole workflow, but nearby sites
keep fast, stable links to each other even when the cloud link
dies; federating them creates a small local cloud. The price is
a placement decision for every task, and every placement
decision ships data across a link.

What makes this problem genuinely hard are two properties that
interact. First, \emph{data movement rivals computation}: every DAG edge carries
an intermediate data volume $D_{ji}$, and when the producing
task $j$ and consuming task $i$ run on different resources, that
data crosses a link of bandwidth $B_{ba}$ at cost
$D_{ji}/B_{ba}$---comparable to a task's own execution time. A
``fast'' remote node can lose more time receiving its input than
it saves executing. Second, \emph{accuracy is a tradeable
resource}: tasks in these workflows are approximate---running a
fraction $\ell$ of the work costs $\ell$ of the time and returns
$\ell$ of the accuracy. A flood model at 80\% fidelity still
warns in time. The two choices interact: placement decides the
transfers, fractions decide the runtimes, and both compete for
the same deadline.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{disaster-response-dag.png}
\caption{The scheduling problem at a glance: the task DAG (left)
is mapped onto the infrastructure graph (right). Placing $T_4$
remotely makes edge $(T_3,T_4)$ pay $D_{34}/B$ across the link;
the same transfer is free if the tasks are co-located.}
\label{fig:dag}
\end{figure}

All of this still assumes the schedule is computed once, before
execution. In a disaster zone that assumption fails hardest:
power loss, a severed link, or hardware failure can kill a whole
fog resource mid-workflow, stranding every unfinished task
assigned to it. Recomputing the complete schedule is too slow,
moves tasks that were running correctly, discards completed
work, and adds migration traffic the degraded network cannot
afford. The sensible move is to repair only what broke.

The work therefore falls into three objectives, one per stage of
the research:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=!,labelwidth=3.4cm]
\item[Objective (a).] Formulate the joint data-aware scheduling
problem for DAG workflows on federated fog---placement, per-edge
data movement, per-link bandwidth, and approximate execution
under a deadline---and build schedulers to solve it.
\emph{(Completed; published at IEEE Cloud Summit
2025~\cite{gupta2025efficient}.)}
\item[Objective (b).] Make the model and its evaluation honest
and measurable: an accuracy bound as a reference, a retry-once
failure model, a bandwidth-degradation study, and quantified
scalability limits. \emph{(Completed; manuscript in
preparation~\cite{gupta2026dataaware,gupta2026code}.)}
\item[Objective (c).] Design a dynamic partial-repartitioning
method that repairs only the affected region of a workflow after
a resource-level failure. \emph{(This proposal.)}
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Summary of proposed solutions.}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=!,labelwidth=3.4cm]
\item[Solution (a).] \textbf{Joint allocation model + four
schedulers.} A mixed-integer linear program couples placement,
data movement, and execution fractions under a deadline, with
correctness proofs; three scalable methods---LP relaxation with
randomized rounding, a data-aware greedy, and a genetic
algorithm---share the same cost model.
\item[Solution (b).] \textbf{A measurable, stress-tested
platform.} A maximum-accuracy bound lets every method be scored
against a reference; a retry-once failure model separates
accuracy from deadline feasibility; a bandwidth-degradation
sweep with a bandwidth-blind baseline shows that a scheduler
can report a perfect score while producing a late schedule; and
each solver's breaking point is measured rather than assumed.
\item[Solution (c).] \textbf{Minimal-region repair.} On
resource failure, a boundary rule grows the smallest affected
subgraph from the stranded tasks; a region solver repartitions
only that subgraph under a recovery cost model; incremental
shortest-path updates restore communication paths for moved
tasks; and the workflow resumes without restarting completed or
unaffected work.
\end{description}

The rest of the document follows that order. Section~2 reviews
related work, structured around the three objectives. Section~3
describes the methodology for each of them. Section~4 presents
preliminary results and publications. Section~5 covers what
remains and the milestones. Section~6 concludes.

\section{Background and Related Works}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews the literature relevant to the three
objectives in turn.

\subsection{DAG task scheduling on heterogeneous and federated
systems}

The reference point for DAG scheduling on heterogeneous machines
is HEFT~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft}, which ranks tasks by upward
rank and places each where it finishes earliest. CPOP appears in
the same study, and PEFT~\cite{arabnejad2014peft} refines the
template with an optimistic cost table. These list schedulers
share three assumptions that break in our setting: transfers
appear only as static edge weights, tasks always execute
completely, and the plan is fixed at submission. Workflow
scheduling on grids and clouds is surveyed by Yu and
Buyya~\cite{yu2005taxonomy}; deadline-constrained variants such
as Abrishami et al.~\cite{abrishami2013deadline} partition the
workflow and distribute the deadline across partitions, but
again assume atomic tasks and stable resources. Characterization
work by Juve et al.~\cite{juve2013workflows} provides the
structural profiles of real scientific workflows---Montage,
CyberShake, LIGO---that motivate our layered-DAG generator.

In the federated-fog tier, schedulers must additionally respect
eligibility (not every task can run everywhere), limited
capacity, and the possibility that the cloud path is unusable.
Our first objective~\cite{gupta2025efficient} builds in this
line: it keeps the list-scheduling template but prices each
transfer by the \emph{actual} link bandwidth between the chosen
nodes, rather than a static edge weight.

\subsection{Data-aware and approximate scheduling}

Fog and edge schedulers manage capacity, eligibility, and
heterogeneity across the compute
continuum~\cite{mouradian2018fog,yi2015fog}. Placement policies
increasingly price data locality, but rarely model per-link
bandwidth explicitly, treat tasks as atomic, and decide the
schedule up front.

Approximate computing, surveyed by
Mittal~\cite{mittal2016approx}, trades result quality for time
or energy via loop truncation, reduced precision, or early
termination---making accuracy a schedulable resource, exactly
what tight deadlines need. However, that literature assumes data
is already where it needs to be; inter-node transfer cost is not
part of the trade. Our second objective~\cite{gupta2026dataaware}
joins the two: per-task execution fractions priced against
per-edge data volumes and per-link bandwidth, measured against
an explicit accuracy bound and a bandwidth-blind baseline that
exposes what ignoring the links costs.

\subsection{Failure recovery, rescheduling, and dynamic graph
updates}

The closest literature to the third objective is
recovery-oriented. Sakellariou and
Zhao~\cite{sakellariou2004rescheduling} reconsider parts of a
workflow at carefully chosen rescheduling points, but remap the
workflow globally at each point. Yang et
al.~\cite{yang2024multiagent} reschedule in fog systems under
uncertain events; Lee et al.~\cite{lee2019fognetwork} study task
redistribution when fog resources join and leave. Classic fault
tolerance---checkpoint/restart and replication---recovers lost
work at recompute or replication cost but never minimizes the
repair region.

On the graph side, Riazi et al.~\cite{riazi2018sssp} and Khanda
et al.~\cite{khanda2021sssp} update a single-source
shortest-path tree after a graph change, touching only the paths
affected by the update, and Srinivasan et
al.~\cite{srinivasan2023scc} apply the same incremental
philosophy to strongly connected components. None of this
machinery has been applied to workflow repartitioning.

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Objective (a): joint data-aware allocation ---
completed}

\emph{Model.} The workflow is a task DAG $G_T=(T,E)$; task $T_i$
has execution time $t_{ia}$ on resource $f_a$, full-execution
accuracy $A_i$, an eligibility set $P_i\subseteq R$, and an
execution fraction $\ell_i\in[0,1]$ costing $\ell_i$ of the time
and returning $\ell_i$ of the accuracy. The infrastructure is a
resource graph $G_R=(R,L)$ whose links $(b,a)$ carry bandwidth
$B_{ba}$. The communication delay of edge $(j,i)$ is
\begin{equation}
c_{ji}^{ba} =
\begin{cases}
D_{ji}/B_{ba} & \text{if } b \neq a,\\
0 & \text{if } b = a \text{ (co-located).}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:comm}
\end{equation}
The problem---place each task on one eligible node, choose its
fraction, respect precedence-plus-data-arrival order, finish by
$t_{\max}$, maximize mean accuracy---is NP-hard even on
unrelated parallel machines.

\emph{Solvers.} Four schedulers share this cost model so that
differences are algorithmic, not accounting
(Figure~\ref{fig:methods}): (i) an exact MILP with a big-$M$
linearization of the product terms and a five-lemma correctness
proof; (ii) an LP relaxation with randomized rounding---relax
assignments to $[0,1]$, sample each task's node, re-solve for
fractions; (iii) a data-aware greedy with priority
$\beta A_i+(1-\beta)\,CP_i$ and earliest-finish placement, which
on a deadline miss retries with a lower global fraction; and
(iv) a genetic algorithm whose chromosome encodes assignment and
fractions together, with a co-location repair for heavy edges.

\subsection{Objective (b): a measurable, stress-tested platform
--- completed}

The second objective hardened the foundation in four ways.
(i)~\emph{Bound.} A maximum-accuracy bound---the score
achievable if every task ran fully on its best eligible node
with instantaneous data arrival---gives every method a
reference at every operating point. (ii)~\emph{Failure
semantics.} A retry-once model reruns a failed task in place and
keeps its accuracy only if the retry still meets the deadline,
which cleanly separates accuracy from feasibility.
(iii)~\emph{Bandwidth degradation.} Scaling every link from
$1.0\times$ down to $0.05\times$ nominal, with a bandwidth-blind
baseline that ignores transfer cost, isolates exactly what
data-awareness buys. (iv)~\emph{Scalability limits.} Each
solver's breaking point is measured: the MILP near 800
assignment variables, the LP near $10^5$ constraint rows at
1,000 tasks, and the GA under a fixed search budget. Structural
graph generation is verified to $10^6$ vertices.

\subsection{Objective (c): dynamic partial repartitioning ---
proposed}
\label{sec:objc}

\emph{The failure event.} At time $\tau$, resource
$r^{*}\in R$ fails and is removed from $G_R$. The task set
partitions into $T^{\mathrm{done}}$, $T^{\mathrm{run}}$, and
$T^{\mathrm{pend}}$; the \emph{stranded set} is
$S=\pi^{-1}(r^{*})\cap(T^{\mathrm{run}}\cup T^{\mathrm{pend}})$.
Two invariants govern everything that follows: tasks in
$T^{\mathrm{done}}$ never restart, and assignments of unaffected
tasks never move.

\emph{Formal problem.} Given $G_T$, the surviving infrastructure
$G_R\setminus\{r^{*}\}$, the original schedule $\pi$, the task
states at $\tau$, and $t_{\max}$: find an affected region
$A\supseteq S$ and a repair assignment
$\pi':A\rightarrow R\setminus\{r^{*}\}$ minimizing the recovery
cost
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{rec}} =
\underbrace{\Delta_{\mathrm{makespan}}}_{\text{added finish
time}} + w_1\cdot\underbrace{|\pi'\neq\pi|}_{\text{tasks moved}}
+ w_2\cdot\underbrace{\text{bytes re-shipped}}_{\text{data
movement}},
\label{eq:crec}
\end{equation}
subject to the invariants above, deadline feasibility, and
minimal accuracy loss. Complete repartition is the special case
$A=T$; the claim to test is that for realistic failures
$|A|\ll|T|$.

\emph{Stage 1 --- Initial partition.} Partition $G_T$ and assign
groups to resources under execution time, capacity,
communication cost, and deadline, reusing the schedulers of
objectives (a) and (b).

\emph{Stage 2 --- Failure detection.} Monitoring marks $r^{*}$
failed; the system identifies the stranded set $S$.

\emph{Stage 3 --- Boundary rule and affected-subgraph extraction
(RQ1).} Construct $A$ by expansion from $S$: repeatedly admit a
neighbor task $v\notin A$ while $v$ fails a \emph{stay-test}:
\begin{align}
\text{deadline:}\quad & \hat{y}_v(\pi) \;>\; \tau + s_v(\tau),
\label{eq:stay-dl}\\
\text{locality:}\quad & \min_{P \in \mathrm{paths}(u \to v)}
\; D_{uv}/B_P \;>\; \delta_v,
\label{eq:stay-loc}
\end{align}
where $\hat{y}_v(\pi)$ is $v$'s finish time under the original
schedule, $s_v(\tau)$ the slack remaining at $\tau$, and
$\delta_v$ a per-task locality budget derived from its
successors' start times. Expansion stops when no neighbor fails
a test or $|A|$ reaches a cap bounding repair effort. The
output is the affected subgraph plus its \emph{boundary
pins}---stationary tasks whose data-arrival times constrain the
repair.

\emph{Stage 4 --- Replacement-resource selection.} From
$R\setminus\{r^{*}\}$ select nodes with the capacity and memory
for $A$, ranked by shortest-path distance to the resources
hosting $A$'s parents and children.

\emph{Stage 5 --- Repartition the region (RQ2).} Repair is a
smaller instance of the same optimization family---deliberately,
so the Stage-1 schedulers become region solvers (greedy for
millisecond-scale repair, MILP as reference on small regions):
\begin{align}
\min_{\pi':\,A\rightarrow R\setminus\{r^{*}\}}\quad
& C_{\mathrm{rec}}(\pi') \label{eq:repair}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \pi'(v)\in P_v\cap\bigl(R\setminus\{r^{*}\}\bigr)
  && \forall\, v\in A, \notag\\
& y_j + c_{ji}^{\pi'(j)\,\pi'(v)} \;\le\; M_v
  && \forall\, (j,v)\in E\cap A, \notag\\
& a_{u\rightarrow v} \text{ fixed}
  && \forall\, (u,v):\; u\notin A,\; v\in A, \notag\\
& y_v \;\le\; t_{\max} && \forall\, v\in A. \notag
\end{align}
The third constraint is the boundary-pin condition: arrival
times from stationary predecessors are constants, so the region
sees the rest of the DAG as a fixed interface.

\emph{Stage 6 --- Assignment and incremental path update (RQ3).}
Assign new partitions, then update communication paths between
moved and stationary tasks incrementally, adapting the dynamic
single-source shortest-path updates of
\cite{riazi2018sssp,khanda2021sssp}. Recomputation per source
costs $O(|L|+|R|\log|R|)$; the incremental update touches only
the $\Theta(|A|)$ moved tasks and the paths through $r^{*}$, so
recovery cost scales with the damage, not the infrastructure.
This is a novel application of dynamic-graph machinery.

\emph{Stage 7 --- Resume.} Execution resumes from the last
successful point: completed outputs are kept, unaffected tasks
run undisturbed, repaired tasks start when their inputs arrive.

\section{Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Objective (a) --- published}

The Cloud Summit study~\cite{gupta2025efficient} established the
formulation and compared the four schedulers across deadline
slack, federation size, workflow size, and task-failure rate:
the MILP is the quality reference; relaxation and the GA track
it most closely; the data-aware greedy is the low-overhead
option under pressure.

\subsection{Objective (b) --- completed}

The data-aware study~\cite{gupta2026dataaware} answers five
questions, each isolated by a sweep over five fresh instances
per point:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=!,labelwidth=2.6cm]
\item[Slack] ($\alpha\in[1.0,2.0]$): at $\alpha=1.0$ the
MILP leads (1.38 vs.\ bound 1.43); from $\alpha=1.2$ the greedy
reaches the bound. Method choice matters most exactly where the
deadline binds (Figure~\ref{fig:slack}).
\item[Federation] ($N\in\{2,\dots,10\}$): MILP and
greedy match the bound at every size; GA and rounding trail up
to $\sim$8\%. Capacity buys feasibility, not accuracy.
\item[Scale] ($n\in\{10,100,1000\}$): at $n=1000$ the
greedy holds the bound in 0.15\,s; rounding costs $\sim$93\,s
per LP re-solve; the GA falls to 1.10 in $\sim$159\,s.
\item[Failures] (rate $0$--$0.5$, retry-once): scores
stay flat but feasibility separates the methods---greedy 100\%
vs.\ rounding $\sim$20\% at rate 0.5, because early finishes
leave room for retries.
\item[Bandwidth] ($\times 1.0\to 0.05$): the
bandwidth-blind baseline reports bound-level scores at every
level while its schedules turn infeasible (80\% at $0.5\times$,
0\% below $0.2\times$); data-aware methods trade accuracy for
feasibility and degrade gracefully
(Figure~\ref{fig:bw}).
\end{description}

\section{Remaining Work and Milestones}
\label{sec:remaining}

\subsection{Objective (b) --- revisions in progress}

The data-aware study is written and formatted to the target
venue (IEEE conference template, double-blind). The manuscript
is currently being revised before submission.

\subsection{Objective (c) --- the proposed work}

Three research questions decompose the proposal, each mapped to
a stage of Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1 --- Boundary (Stage 3).] Formalize and validate the
stay-tests of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:stay-dl}--\eqref{eq:stay-loc}:
prove or empirically establish that the region they grow is
minimal yet large enough to repair, and measure how the region
grows with failure location and remaining slack.
\item[RQ2 --- Repair (Stages 4--5).] Implement the region
solver for the problem of Eq.~\eqref{eq:repair}, from a
greedy millisecond-scale repair to MILP repair as the small-
region reference.
\item[RQ3 --- Paths (Stage 6).] Adapt incremental SPT updates
to moved-task paths and measure where they beat full
recomputation.
\end{description}

Evaluation will compare against complete repartition, greedy
rescue, a no-repair static baseline, and MILP repair on small
cases, over failure location (critical path vs.\ peripheral),
failure count, remaining slack, workflow size, and federation
size. Three hypotheses: \textbf{H1} recovery time scales with
$|A|$ not $|T|$; \textbf{H2} tasks moved and bytes re-shipped
are far below the baselines at equal feasibility;
\textbf{H3} repair quality stays near the MILP reference on
small cases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

This research develops a complete treatment of data-intensive
DAG scheduling on federated fog, structured as three papers on
one arc. The first established the joint model---placement,
per-edge data, per-link bandwidth, approximate execution---with
an exact solver, three scalable methods, and proofs. The second
made that platform measurable: a bound, failure semantics,
bandwidth degradation, and honest scale limits, all public as a
reproducible artifact. The third, proposed here, removes the
static assumption: when a resource fails mid-workflow, repair
the smallest affected region instead of rescheduling the world.
Together the three contributions move the field from \emph{where
should the workflow run} to \emph{what it costs to keep it
running when the infrastructure fails}---with the platform, cost
model, schedulers, and baselines for that final step already
built.
