\begin{abstract}
Time-critical workflows---flood forecasting, wildfire tracking,
evacuation planning---run as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) on
federated fog resources near the event, because the link to the
cloud is degraded or unavailable exactly when the workflow is
needed most. My completed work established how to place such
workflows well: a data-aware formulation coupling placement,
per-edge data movement, and per-task approximate execution,
solved by an exact mixed-integer program and three scalable
schedulers. All of that work assumes the schedule is decided
once, before execution. This proposal removes that assumption.
When a whole fog resource fails mid-workflow, we propose to
identify the smallest affected region of the task DAG,
repartition and reassign only that region to surviving
resources, update only the communication paths that changed,
and resume---never restarting completed work or disturbing
unaffected assignments. The goal is faster recovery with less
disturbance than complete repartitioning, at near-reference
schedule quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Workflows that respond to disasters, such as flood forecasting,
wildfire tracking, and evacuation planning, run as directed
acyclic graphs (DAGs) of dependent tasks. In a federated fog
system these tasks are placed on resources close to the event,
because the link to the cloud may be slow or unavailable exactly
when the workflow is needed most. A forecast that arrives after
the water is a forecast nobody uses: the deadline is part of the
specification, not a preference.

My previous work studied how to place these workflows well. Our
IEEE Cloud Summit 2025 paper defined the allocation problem and
compared four scheduling methods~\cite{gupta2025efficient}. My
current work extends that study: it makes the model data-aware,
adds a measurable accuracy bound, and tests the schedulers under
deadline slack, federation growth, workflow growth, task
failures, and link-bandwidth
degradation~\cite{gupta2026dataaware,gupta2026code}.

All of this work has one common assumption: the schedule is
decided once, before execution starts. In a real deployment this
is not enough. A fog resource can fail while the workflow is
running---in a disaster zone, power loss, a severed link, or
hardware failure are expected events, not edge cases---and every
unfinished task assigned to that resource is stranded.
Recomputing the complete schedule takes too much time, moves
tasks that are already running correctly, discards completed
work, and increases communication and migration costs.

This proposal presents a different method. When a resource
fails, we identify only the part of the task DAG that is affected
by the failure, repartition and reassign only that part to
available resources, and leave everything else unchanged. The
remainder of this document defines the problem formally
(Section~\ref{sec:problem}), reviews related work and states the
gap (Section~\ref{sec:related}), sets out the research questions
and objectives (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}), details the methodology
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), summarizes the preliminary results
that the platform already provides (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}),
describes the evaluation plan (Section~\ref{sec:eval}), the
timeline (Section~\ref{sec:timeline}), risks
(Section~\ref{sec:risks}), and expected contributions
(Section~\ref{sec:contrib}).

\section{Background and completed work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Published work: IEEE Cloud Summit 2025}

In~\cite{gupta2025efficient} we introduced the task-allocation
problem for DAG workflows on federated fog systems. The model
connects task placement with data movement: if a task and its
predecessor run on different nodes, the data on the edge $D_{ji}$
must travel over a link with bandwidth $B_{ba}$, which costs
$D_{ji}/B_{ba}$ time. We built a mixed-integer linear program
(MILP) with correctness proofs, and three scalable methods: an LP
relaxation with randomized rounding, a data-aware greedy
scheduler, and a genetic algorithm. We compared them under
deadline slack, number of fog nodes, workflow size, and task
failure rate.

\subsection{Current work: data-aware scheduling}

In~\cite{gupta2026dataaware} I improved the same model and made
the experiments more complete. The work adds a maximum-accuracy
bound so each method can be checked against a clear reference; a
retry-once failure model, which separates accuracy from deadline
feasibility; a bandwidth-degradation experiment with a
bandwidth-blind baseline, which shows that a scheduler can look
good on accuracy while producing late schedules; and scalability
measurements that show where each method stops working (the MILP
near 800 assignment variables, the LP near $10^5$ constraints at
1000 tasks, and the genetic algorithm under a fixed search
budget). The main lesson is that the choice of scheduler matters
most when the deadline is tight, and the simple data-aware greedy
is the only method that is both fast and close to the bound at
1000 tasks.

The code, data, executed notebook, figures, and slides are public
at \url{https://github.com/gayanigupta/data-aware-scheduling}~\cite{gupta2026code}.

\subsection{What is still missing}
\label{sec:missing}

Both studies handle failures only at the task level: a failed
task is retried once on the same node. Neither study answers what
to do when a whole resource fails during execution. That gap is
the problem of this proposal: the failure granularity moves from
\emph{task retry} to \emph{resource loss}, and the scheduling
granularity must therefore move from \emph{whole-DAG placement}
to \emph{minimal-region repair}.

\section{Related work and the research gap}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{DAG scheduling on heterogeneous systems}

The reference point is HEFT~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft}, which ranks
tasks by upward rank and places each task where it finishes
earliest. HEFT and its descendants (CPOP, PEFT) share three
assumptions that break in our setting: transfers appear only as
static edge weights, tasks always execute completely, and the
plan is fixed at submission time. Our completed work keeps the
list-scheduling template but prices transfers by the actual link
bandwidth and makes execution approximate.

\subsection{Fog and edge scheduling}

Fog schedulers manage capacity, eligibility, and heterogeneity
across the compute continuum~\cite{mouradian2018fog}. Placement
policies in this literature increasingly price data locality, but
rarely model per-link bandwidth explicitly, treat tasks as
atomic (run completely or not at all), and decide the schedule up
front---handling failures by restart rather than repair.

\subsection{Approximate computing}

Approximate computing trades result quality for time or energy
(loop truncation, reduced precision, early termination), which
makes accuracy a schedulable resource---exactly what tight
deadlines need. However, this literature generally assumes data
is already where it needs to be; inter-node transfer cost is not
part of the trade. Our model joins approximation with per-edge
data volumes and per-link bandwidth.

\subsection{Failure recovery, rescheduling, and dynamic graphs}

The closest literature to this proposal is recovery-oriented.
Sakellariou and Zhao~\cite{sakellariou2004rescheduling} reconsider
parts of a workflow at carefully chosen points but remap the
workflow globally at each point. Yang et
al.~\cite{yang2024multiagent} reschedule in fog systems when
uncertain events occur. Lee et al.~\cite{lee2019fognetwork} study
task redistribution when fog resources join and leave. On the
graph side, Riazi et al.~\cite{riazi2018sssp} and Khanda et
al.~\cite{khanda2021sssp} update a single-source shortest-path
tree after a graph change, touching only the paths affected by
the update---but this machinery has never been applied to
workflow repartitioning. Checkpoint/restart and replication
recover lost work at recompute or replication cost; neither
minimizes the repair region.

\subsection{The gap}

Each capability exists in isolation, but no work couples an
infrastructure-node failure to the smallest affected region of
the task DAG:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
Capability & HEFT/classic & Fog schedulers &
Our completed work & Proposed \\
\midrule
Placement on heterogeneous nodes & yes & yes & yes & yes \\
Per-edge data + per-link bandwidth & no & partial & yes & yes \\
Approximate execution fractions & no & no & yes & yes \\
Recovery after resource failure & restart & reschedule all
& retry task & \textbf{repair region only} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The proposed work fills the last column: map a resource failure
to the smallest affected DAG region, repartition only that
region, and update communication paths incrementally.

\section{Problem definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{System model}

The workflow is a task DAG $G_T=(T,E)$. Task $T_i$ has execution
time $t_{ia}$ on resource $f_a$, full-execution accuracy $A_i$,
an eligibility set $P_i\subseteq R$, and can run to a fraction
$\ell_i\in[0,1]$, costing $\ell_i$ of the time and returning
$\ell_i$ of the accuracy. Each edge $(j,i)\in E$ carries $D_{ji}$
bytes of dependency data; when $j$ and $i$ are placed on
different nodes, that data crosses a link of bandwidth $B_{ba}$
at cost $D_{ji}/B_{ba}$.

The infrastructure is a resource graph $G_R=(R,L)$ whose nodes
$f_a\in R$ carry processing capacity, memory, current workload,
and availability, and whose links $(b,a)\in L$ carry delay,
bandwidth, and transfer cost. A schedule is a partition
$\pi:T\rightarrow R$ assigning each task to one eligible
resource, plus per-task execution fractions, subject to
precedence-plus-data-arrival order and a global deadline
$t_{\max}$.

\subsection{The failure event}

During execution the infrastructure is monitored. At time
$\tau$, resource $r^{*}\in R$ fails: it is removed from $G_R$
and every task assigned to it that has not completed can no
longer continue. At that instant the task set partitions into
$T^{\text{done}}$, $T^{\text{run}}$, and $T^{\text{pend}}$.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Stranded set}
$S=\pi^{-1}(r^{*})\cap(T^{\text{run}}\cup T^{\text{pend}})$:
unfinished tasks on the failed resource.
\item \textbf{Invariant}: tasks in $T^{\text{done}}$ never
restart; assignments of unaffected tasks never move.
\item \textbf{Affected region} $A\supseteq S$: the smallest
subgraph of $G_T$ that must be repartitioned---$S$ plus any
dependent tasks that can no longer meet the deadline or retain
data locality if left in place.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problem statement}

\textbf{Dynamic partial repartitioning.} Given $G_T$, the
surviving infrastructure $G_R\setminus\{r^{*}\}$, the original
schedule $\pi$, the task states at time $\tau$, and the deadline
$t_{\max}$: find an affected region $A$ and a repair assignment
$\pi':A\rightarrow R\setminus\{r^{*}\}$ that restores a feasible
schedule while minimizing the recovery cost

\[
C_{\text{rec}} = \underbrace{\Delta_{\text{makespan}}}_{\text{added
finish time}} + w_1\cdot\underbrace{|\pi'\neq\pi|}_{\text{tasks
moved}} + w_2\cdot\underbrace{\text{bytes re-shipped}}_{\text{data
movement}} ,
\]

subject to: completed tasks never restart; tasks outside $A$
keep their original assignment; the repaired schedule meets the
deadline; and accuracy degrades as little as possible. A
complete repartition is the special case $A=T$; the claim is
that for realistic failures $|A|\ll|T|$.

\section{Research questions and objectives}
\label{sec:rqs}

\textbf{Main research question.} How can we quickly repartition
and reschedule only the affected part of a DAG workflow after a
fog resource fails, while reducing recovery time, communication
cost, task movement, and workflow completion time?

\noindent This decomposes into three research questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1 --- Boundary.] What is the smallest region $A$ of the
task DAG that must be repartitioned? Which dependents of
stranded tasks must move with them---for deadline slack or data
locality---and which can safely stay?
\item[RQ2 --- Repair.] How should $A$ be repartitioned onto
surviving resources so that the repaired schedule meets the
deadline at minimum recovery cost, while accuracy degrades as
little as possible?
\item[RQ3 --- Paths.] How can communication paths between moved
tasks and the rest of the DAG be updated incrementally instead
of recomputed, so that recovery stays fast on large
infrastructure graphs?
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Objectives.}
O1: a principled boundary rule for $A$ (RQ1).
O2: a repartitioning algorithm under the recovery cost model
(RQ2).
O3: incremental communication-path updates driven by dynamic
shortest-path machinery (RQ3).
O4: evidence---against complete repartition, greedy rescue, and
MILP repair---that recovery time and disturbance shrink while
quality stays near reference.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

The method is a seven-stage pipeline; Stages 1--2 use existing
machinery, Stages 3--6 are the proposed work, and Stage 7
defines the execution semantics.

\subsection{Stage 1 --- Initial partition}

Partition $G_T$ into groups of connected tasks and assign them
to suitable fog resources under execution time, capacity,
communication cost, and the deadline. For this stage we reuse
the schedulers already built and evaluated in the current work:
the MILP for small instances, the data-aware greedy for large
ones.

\subsection{Stage 2 --- Failure detection}

Monitoring marks resource $r^{*}$ failed during execution and
the system identifies the affected node in $G_R$ and the
stranded set $S$ in $G_T$.

\subsection{Stage 3 --- Boundary rule and affected-subgraph
extraction (RQ1)}

Construct $A$ by expansion from $S$: start with $A=S$, then
repeatedly admit a neighbor task $v\notin A$ while $v$ fails a
\emph{stay-test}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Deadline test:} $v$ can no longer finish within
the slack remaining at $\tau$ if it stays (e.g., its input must
now cross a slow path, or its parent moved onto the critical
path);
\item \textbf{Locality test:} $v$'s input data is stranded on a
link too slow to serve it in time ($D/B$ too large).
\end{itemize}

Expansion stops when no neighbor fails a test or when $|A|$
reaches a cap that bounds repair effort. The output is the
affected subgraph---the only part of $G_T$ that is
repartitioned---with its \emph{boundary pins}: the tasks that
stayed, whose data-arrival times constrain the repair. The
research deliverable is a rule that provably stays minimal while
remaining large enough to repair well; we will study its
sensitivity to failure location and remaining slack
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\subsection{Stage 4 --- Replacement-resource selection}

From $R\setminus\{r^{*}\}$ select nodes with the processing
capacity and memory to run tasks of $A$, using shortest-path
information toward the resources that host $A$'s parents and
children, so replacements keep data transfers short.

\subsection{Stage 5 --- Repartition the affected region (RQ2)}

Repartition $A$ under the recovery cost model: execution time,
communication delay, transferred data, resource capacity, the
deadline, move cost, and load balance. Repair is a smaller
instance of the same optimization family as the original
scheduling problem, which is deliberate: the completed work's
schedulers become \emph{region solvers}---the data-aware greedy
for millisecond-scale repair, the MILP as the quality reference
where the region is small. Boundary pins appear as fixed
data-arrival times the repair must respect.

\subsection{Stage 6 --- Assignment and incremental path update
(RQ3)}

Assign the new partitions to the selected resources, changing as
little of the original schedule as possible, then update the
communication paths between reassigned and stationary tasks.
Rather than recomputing all-pairs shortest paths on $G_R$---too
slow mid-workflow---we adapt the dynamic single-source
shortest-path updates of Riazi, Bhowmick, and
colleagues~\cite{riazi2018sssp,khanda2021sssp}: only the paths
touching the deleted node and the moved tasks are updated. This
is what makes partial repair fast enough to matter, and it is a
novel application of dynamic-graph machinery.

\subsection{Stage 7 --- Resume}

The workflow resumes from the last successful execution point:
completed tasks keep their outputs, unaffected tasks keep their
assignments, and repaired tasks start once their inputs are in
place.

\section{Preliminary results}
\label{sec:prelim}

The platform this proposal builds on already exists and is
published or submitted:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A proved formulation} coupling placement,
per-edge data volumes, per-link bandwidth, and per-task
approximate execution under a hard deadline, with a
solver-ready linearization and correctness proofs
(Lemmas 1--5, Theorem 1)~\cite{gupta2025efficient,gupta2026dataaware}.
\item \textbf{Four schedulers on one cost model} (MILP, LP
randomized rounding, data-aware greedy, GA) plus a
max-accuracy bound used as the yardstick in every experiment.
\item \textbf{Measured evidence} that scheduler choice matters
exactly where the deadline binds; that the data-aware greedy is
the only method both fast (0.15\,s) and bound-level at 1000
tasks; that early-finish headroom is what keeps schedules
feasible under failures; and that a bandwidth-blind scheduler
reports bound-level scores while producing late
schedules~\cite{gupta2026dataaware}.
\item \textbf{A reproducible artifact} (code, data, executed
notebook, figures) at a public repository~\cite{gupta2026code},
including graph generation verified to $10^6$ vertices---the
scales the failure-injection experiments will need.
\end{itemize}

The missing piece is precisely the proposal: the platform's
failure model is retry-once-in-place, and every schedule is
decided up front.

\section{Evaluation plan}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Baselines}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Complete repartition:} repartition and reschedule
the whole workflow---the na\"ive upper bound on disturbance.
\item \textbf{Greedy rescue:} reassign stranded tasks one by one
to the first suitable resource---the cheapest possible repair.
\item \textbf{Static/no repair:} keep whatever survives---the
lower bound on quality.
\item \textbf{MILP repair} on small instances---the quality
reference, supplied by the existing stack.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}

Failure-recovery time (detection $\rightarrow$ resume), workflow
completion time and deadline-success rate, communication cost
and bytes re-shipped, number of tasks moved, energy use, and the
quality of the repaired schedule against the bound.

\subsection{Failure injection}

We vary what makes recovery hard: \emph{location} (a resource on
the critical path vs.\ a peripheral resource), \emph{count}
(single vs.\ multi-resource failure), \emph{timing} (how much
deadline slack remains at $\tau$), \emph{workflow size}
($n\in\{10,100,1000\}$), and \emph{federation size}
($N\in\{2,\ldots,10\}$). Recovery is easy with plenty of slack;
the hard and interesting case is a late failure on the critical
path.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[H1.] Recovery time grows with $|A|$, not $|T|$: partial
repair is asymptotically and practically faster than complete
repartition.
\item[H2.] Tasks moved and bytes re-shipped are much smaller
than every baseline at equal deadline feasibility.
\item[H3.] Repaired-schedule quality stays within a small margin
of MILP repair on small instances.
\end{description}

\section{Risks and limitations}
\label{sec:risks}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Boundary degeneration.} The boundary rule might
expand to most of the DAG, degenerating to complete
repartition. Mitigation: the cap on $|A|$ plus the sensitivity
study over failure location and remaining slack.
\item \textbf{Incremental-update payoff.} Incremental
shortest-path updates may not pay off on small infrastructure
graphs. Mitigation: measure the break-even point; keep full
recomputation as a baseline.
\item \textbf{Repair quality.} Region repair may trail MILP
quality. Mitigation: a hybrid strategy---greedy repair at
scale, MILP repair where the region is small.
\end{itemize}

\section{Expected contributions and summary}
\label{sec:contrib}

The three works form one line of research. The first paper built
the model and compared four ways to solve the static problem.
The current work made the model more complete and showed where
each method stops working. This proposal changes two things at
once: the failure granularity, from retrying a task to losing a
whole resource; and the scheduling granularity, from placing the
whole DAG to repairing only the smallest affected part. The
expected contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item a principled boundary rule for the smallest affected DAG
region after a resource failure (RQ1);
\item a minimal-region repartitioning method under a recovery
cost model that preserves completed and unaffected work (RQ2);
\item the first application of incremental shortest-path updates
to workflow recovery (RQ3); and
\item evidence that partial repair recovers faster with less
disturbance than complete repartition at near-reference quality
(O4).
\end{enumerate}

The platform, the cost model, the schedulers, and the baselines
already exist, so the new work can build on them directly.
